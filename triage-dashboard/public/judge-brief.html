1.Backend architecture

The whole system is three layers.

Browser  ──▶  Next.js (dashboard + /api proxy)  ──▶  FastAPI scoring service
  • The browser shows one screen: the caseload, ranked by who needs a caseworker first.
  • Next.js serves that screen and forwards every /api/* call to the scoring service, so the dashboard only ever talks to relative URLs, so there is no CORS and nothing to configure.
  • The FastAPI service reads the sensor data, computes each resident's risk, and pushes a live event the second a fall fires.

The home senses. The service scores. The caseworker decides.

What each layer is built on, and why:

LayerWe usedBecause
Scoring servicePython 3.11 + FastAPIThe team is Python, and both datasets are Python-native.
The mathnumpy · scipy · pandasSignal magnitude, baselines, z-scores, all standard numeric work.
Live channelSSE (server-sent events)The dashboard just listens on an EventSource. Simpler than WebSockets, and the demo climax needs a live push.
Front doorNext.js proxies the serviceKeeps the browser on relative URLs. No CORS, no config in the client.
StorageNoneBaselines precompute to a small JSON file. Nothing needs a database for the demo.

We don't train a model. The datasets are realistic streams we compute over, and the detector explains every score it produces.

2.Feature specification: from raw data to the live demo

Why these two datasets?

SisFall gives us 4,505 real accelerometer recordings, 1,798 of them real falls, from 38 people. They were recorded on an ADXL345, the same cheap sensor class a worn bangle carries, and every fall shows up as a sharp spike at impact.

CASAS Aruba is 220 days of one real elderly person living alone, every motion and door event logged as it happened for the better part of a year.

The detector. From SisFall we built the detector — plain calculations for what a fall actually looks like: the free-fall dip, the spike at impact, the stillness after.

Routine. From CASAS we learn routine — what one resident's ordinary day looks like, so we can tell when it breaks.

How a fall is detected

A fall is an ordered sequence:

  1. Free-fall dip: the accelerometer drops below 0.8 g as the body drops.
  2. Impact spike: within half a second, it jumps above 2.3 g when the body hits the floor.
  3. Stillness: for the next ~10 seconds, the person doesn't move.

A dropped phone gives you the spike but not the free-fall before it or the stillness after. Requiring the whole sequence is what keeps ordinary life — sitting down hard, setting a phone on a table — from firing.

Where the numbers came from: we counted them

We didn't set the thresholds by hand and hope. We wrote the detector — dip, then impact, then stillness — pointed it at all 4,505 recordings, and counted: falls caught, false fires. We turned the dials and counted again until it caught 96.2% of real falls. We never dropped a recording or a person from the set; we only measured. The number is counted, not claimed.

Because the recordings are labelled by who performed them, we counted the same thing separately for the seniors:

  • Elderly false alarms: 18.8% — 168 of 893 ordinary-activity recordings from the elderly participants (SE01–SE15) that tripped the detector when nothing was wrong. That's roughly half the young-adult rate of 35.2% (638 of 1,814): elderly movement is slower and less abrupt, so it looks less like a fall.
  • Elderly fall detection: 84.0% — 63 of 75 real elderly falls caught. All 75 came from a single volunteer (SE06, the only elderly participant cleared to perform falls): one body, not fifteen. A promising number on thin evidence, and we won't dress it up.

On CASAS the resident is their own baseline. From 220 days we learned their normal — when they first enter the kitchen, how many night trips they make, their typical door times — and scored each new day as a deviation from that rhythm. No population average, no imported assumption about how a senior "should" live. Each person is measured only against themselves, which is how the routine track works without any training.

How we found the five residents

The dashboard shows a caseload of five — Rajoo, Wong, Lim, Devi, and Goh — each ranked by need. Four are calm. The system pushed Rajoo to the top (0.65) on its own: we handed it 220 days and asked it to find the day the routine broke. It picked 2011-02-19, the day his own pattern fell apart — a long kitchen gap, an early door, fewer night trips. We never marked that day; it found it.

The next feature: the live simulation

The demo's beat is a fall firing mid-shift. Pressing Simulate replays a real recorded SisFall fall into the same detector the caseload runs on, and it rotates through several real traces, so each press is a different body and a different waveform rather than a canned replay. Detection sends a live event down the stream; the fall pins to the top in seconds, flashes, and opens its drill-down. The waveform on screen is the actual trace the score was computed on, so the picture and the numbers agree. A second button injects a dropped phone — a spike with no free-fall or stillness — and the detector correctly stays silent, the specificity you want in a home.

The demo run

Two processes: the FastAPI service and the Next.js dashboard. The caseload loads calm and ranked. Press Simulate; the recorded trace runs through detect_fall, the incident arrives back over SSE, the acute row takes over the top. Only the wristband link is faked — detection, the push, and the re-rank are the real production path.

3.Approach: problem to dashboard

The problem

Alarm buttons and pendants only help while the fallen person can still press them. A senior who falls and goes unconscious presses nothing. The fall becomes a long lie — hours on the floor before anyone thinks to check.

The approach, and the hardware already exists

The home already knows. Routine is a vital sign, and absence of motion is data. The routine track needs the senior to wear or press nothing; ambient motion and door sensors are enough. One optional bangle adds a second layer that catches a fall in seconds instead of hours. All of it is off-the-shelf: the Bangle.js 2 (~S$130) and Aqara motion and contact sensors (~US$20). Singapore already runs senior alert wearables at scale — GovTech's WAAS covers 26,800 seniors across 170 blocks. We're not inventing hardware; we're pointing existing hardware at the right question.

Researching and understanding the data

We calibrated fall detection on real recordings and let each resident self-baseline from their own history. The two tracks stay separate: a fall is physics that transfers across any body and any floor, while routine is self-referential and each person is their own control. Calibration only touches the fall track on SisFall; the routine track is never tuned against a target. Neither track imports a foreign prior, which is why the method carries to Singapore without local training data.

Building the dashboard

One screen, calm by default. Every resident ranked by need, every row explaining itself in one sentence. Every score breaks down into the sensor facts that produced it. Risk and confidence sit on separate axes, so a stale sensor lowers confidence but can never fake a crisis. The system ranks and explains; a human makes the call.

What we don't claim

  • We shrink the time it takes to notice a fall. We don't promise to catch every one.
  • Our fall thresholds were tuned on the whole SisFall set, mostly younger adults, but we measured the senior false-alarm rate separately at 18.8% on 893 elderly recordings, about half the young-adult rate — measured, not extrapolated. The remaining caveats are narrower: the elderly fall data is thin (63 of 75 caught, all from one volunteer), and a lab fall is not a home fall.
  • If a fall is missed, or the bangle is left on the nightstand, the resident still surfaces the slow way: hours of unusual stillness push them up the routine ranking by morning.
  • A genuinely irregular life gets wide baselines and scores less sensitively. We show that as low confidence rather than hiding it.
