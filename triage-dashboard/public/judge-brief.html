Morning Triage: Judge Brief

The plain-language companion to the pitch: how it is built, what the data does, and the problem-to-dashboard story. Everything here is real and runs live.

1.Backend architecture

The whole system is three layers, each with one job.

Browser  ──▶  Next.js (dashboard + /api proxy)  ──▶  FastAPI scoring service
  • The browser shows one screen: the caseload, ranked by who needs a caseworker first.
  • Next.js serves that screen and forwards every /api/* call to the scoring service, so the dashboard only ever talks to relative URLs, which means there is no CORS and nothing to configure.
  • The FastAPI service reads the sensor data, computes each resident's risk, and pushes a live event the second a fall fires.

The home senses. The service scores. The caseworker decides.

How the data reaches the screen, and why through an API. The sensor recordings sit with the scoring service, never in the browser. When the dashboard loads, it asks the service for the caseload over /api/caseload; the service runs the detector and the routine scoring over each resident's inputs and returns the computed scores and ranking as JSON. The browser receives numbers and one-line rationale, never raw signal. The demo caseload is a fixed cast of residents, but none of their scores or ranking are stored: the pipeline recomputes them on every request, and a real deployment feeds the same code live sensor data in place of the fixed inputs. We put scoring behind an API for three reasons. The detector, the calibration, and the dataset loaders are Python built on numpy and scipy, which the browser cannot run. The dashboard stays display-only behind a single data seam, so the screen never computes a score it would then have to defend. And the same seam that serves the morning caseload also carries the live fall re-rank over SSE, so the acute alert arrives through the exact path the caseload already uses.

What each layer is built on, and why:

LayerWe usedBecause
Scoring servicePython 3.11 + FastAPIThe team is Python, and both datasets are Python-native.
The mathnumpy · scipy · pandasSignal magnitude, baselines, z-scores, all standard numeric work.
Live channelSSE (server-sent events)The dashboard just listens on an EventSource. Simpler than WebSockets, and the demo climax needs a live push.

We ship calibrated thresholds rather than a trained model, and the one model we did train is kept as evidence for that choice (section 2). The datasets are realistic streams we compute over, and the detector explains every score it produces.

2.Feature specification: from raw data to the live demo

How we get the data. We use two public datasets. SisFall gives us the falls: 4,505 accelerometer recordings from 38 people, 1,798 of them real falls, all recorded on an ADXL345, the same cheap sensor a worn bangle carries. CASAS Aruba gives us the routine: 220 days of one elderly person living alone, with every motion and door event logged. Before anything runs, fetch_datasets.py checks each file against a pinned sha256 hash, so what sits on disk is provably the published dataset. SisFall arrives organised by person: 38 folders, 23 young adults and 15 elderly, and inside each folder the files are named by type, F for a fall and D for an ordinary activity.

How we filter it. Each dataset gets one filter. On SisFall we filter by participant: calibrate.py --subjects SE keeps the 15 elderly folders to measure seniors on their own, and running it with no filter keeps all 38 pooled for the headline number. On CASAS there is no cohort to split, because it is one person, so the filter is time: we learn that resident's own baseline from their 220 days and score every later day against it. Neither filter imports an outside assumption about how a senior should move or live.

How we use it. From SisFall we build the fall detector, a set of plain calculations for the shape of a fall, and calibrate its thresholds against every recording. From CASAS we build each resident's routine baseline. Both run live inside the scoring service, and the dashboard ranks the caseload from the scores they return.

What a fall looks like

A fall is an ordered sequence:

  1. Free-fall dip: the accelerometer drops below 0.8 g as the body drops.
  2. Impact spike: within half a second, it jumps above 2.3 g when the body hits the floor.
  3. Stillness: for the next ~10 seconds, the person does not move.

A dropped phone gives a spike, but it produces neither the free-fall before it nor the stillness after, which is how we tell a dropped object from a person falling.

How we set the operating point

The detector has four thresholds: how far the signal must dip for free-fall, how hard it must spike for impact, how long the dip must last, and how quickly the impact must follow. We swept 144 combinations of them (four free-fall cutoffs, four impact cutoffs, three dip durations, three impact windows). Every combination ran through all 4,505 recordings and produced its own confusion matrix. We ranked the results by detection first, then by the lower false-alarm rate, because a missed fall costs more than a false one. These were the top rows of that sweep:

The three rows above the highlight lower the impact cutoff to 2.0 g. That catches about 1.4 more points of real falls, but fires on nearly 5 more points of ordinary activity. We took the row at the knee: free-fall below 0.8 g, impact above 2.3 g, a dip of at least 40 ms, and impact within 500 ms. It caught 96.2% of real falls at a 29.8% false-alarm rate on the pooled set, and those four thresholds are the ones the live detector runs. We never dropped a recording or a person from the set.

Where the numbers live. Every headline figure on this page comes off one committed chain: fetch_datasets.py verifies the raw data against pinned sha256 hashes, curate.py bakes the committed feature table, and calibrate.py --json-out writes the sweep results to data/metrics.json, which is checked into the repository. The 96.2%, the 29.8%, and the elderly figures below are the numbers in that file, so re-running the pipeline on any machine reproduces this page.

How we trained the model, and what it told us

What we trained it on. curate.py bakes 500 real SisFall recordings into a committed feature table: 250 falls and 250 ordinary activities, each reduced to four magnitude features (peak impact, free-fall depth, impact energy, post-impact stillness). On that table training.py fits a small logistic regression by batch gradient descent: 4,000 iterations, zero-initialised weights, and no randomness anywhere, so the same numbers come out on every machine. One fit takes 0.050 seconds on a laptop CPU, and the full report below, with every re-fit included, takes 0.45 seconds.

How we split and validated it. The curated table arrives falls first, so a naive cut would test on a single class. We interleave the two classes into a fixed order and hold out the first 25%: 375 recordings train, 125 test, and every number we report is measured on the held-out test set. At that split the model reaches:

  • 80.0% accuracy, 77.9% precision, 84.1% recall on the 125 held-out recordings: 53 falls caught, 10 missed, 15 false alarms, 47 correct calms.
  • The split is not doing the work. Re-fitting at 60/40, 70/30 and 80/20 moves accuracy only between 78.5% and 80.0%.
  • More data does not raise the ceiling. Re-fitting on growing slices of the training set gives 78.4% at 38 recordings and 80.0% at all 375; the learning curve is flat.

What the ceiling means. The four features are magnitudes, and a hard sit can spike past 2 g just as a fall does, so magnitudes alone stop at 80.0%. What separates the two is the sequence, free-fall then impact then stillness, and that sequence is exactly what the threshold detector above reads. Training the model told us where the 16.2-point gap to 96.2% comes from, which is why we ship the ordered thresholds and keep the model as evidence. The recorded run is live on the dashboard's /training page: the convergence charts, the split controls, and the learned coefficients, every number measured from this training run.

The elderly, measured separately

We ran the same detector and the same confusion count on the 15 elderly folders alone. Only one of those participants (SE06) was cleared to perform falls, so the 75 elderly fall recordings all come from that one body, while the 893 ordinary-activity recordings draw on all 15. Counted that way:

  • Elderly false alarms: 18.8%. That is 168 of 893 ordinary-activity recordings from the elderly participants (SE01 to SE15) that tripped the detector when nothing was wrong, roughly half the young-adult rate of 35.2% (638 of 1,814). Elderly movement is slower and less abrupt, so it looks less like a fall.
  • Elderly fall detection: 84.0%, 63 of 75 real elderly falls caught. All 75 came from a single volunteer (SE06, the only elderly participant cleared to perform falls), so this is one body, not fifteen. It is a promising number on thin evidence.

On CASAS each resident is scored against their own history. From 220 days we learn their normal, when they first enter the kitchen, how many night trips they make, their typical door times, and we score each new day as a deviation from that rhythm.

How we found the five residents

The dashboard shows a caseload of five (Rajoo, Wong, Lim, Devi, and Goh), each ranked by need. Four are calm. The system pushed Rajoo to the top (0.65) on its own: we handed it 220 days and asked it to find the day the routine broke. It picked 2011-02-19, the day his own pattern fell apart, with a long kitchen gap, an early door, and fewer night trips. We never marked that day as anomalous.

The camera: a second way to catch the fall

How the camera sees a fall. The /watch page runs MediaPipe pose estimation entirely in the browser. The detection logic is a pure state machine in fallHeuristic.ts, covered by 16 unit tests that replay synthetic landmark sequences, so the rule is testable without a camera. The rule is ordered:

  • A person we have tracked upright goes horizontal within 3 seconds.
  • They then stay still for 3.0 seconds, and the alert fires.
  • A slower transition is a deliberate lie-down and never alarms; after firing, a 10 second cooldown holds.

What we do not pretend. The browser posts the detection to /incidents/cv-detected, and from there the camera is a second acute source feeding the same incident path as the accelerometer: caseload preemption, the SSE push, the Telegram alert. Its incidents carry the sensor label Camera (pose) and the heuristic's own confidence estimate, a 0.55 to 0.85 band computed from transition speed, because the 96.2% figure belongs only to the accelerometer detector. A camera incident also serves no accelerometer waveform; /incidents/trace returns 404 for it, since there is no trace to show. One geometric limit stays. The heuristic measures the torso angle in the image plane of a single camera, so a fall straight toward or away from the lens foreshortens below the angle threshold and can be missed, while a fall across the frame is the case it sees well. A missed camera fall surfaces the slow way, through the routine ranking, the same as a missed bangle fall.

Who fell, by name. Enrollment on /watch is opt-in. We capture front, left, and right angles per person as 128-dimension embeddings, stored in the browser's localStorage and bound to a caseload resident chosen from the roster. No face image is stored, no video leaves the browser, and only the detection event and the matched resident id are sent. Matching runs only while the person is upright, one check roughly every 0.7 seconds, and the binding is sticky:

  • 2 sightings attach a name; 3 sustained sightings of a different person change it.
  • The binding survives 30 seconds with no face visible, which is what carries the name through the fall itself.
  • The fall event then names that resident on the dashboard row and in the Telegram message. Anything unmatched fires the generic alert, and recognition can never block or delay an alert.

The alert leg, and its receipt. A detected fall sends a caregiver alert through a Telegram bot, dispatched off the request thread so a slow or failed send never delays the incident. The bot credentials live only in environment variables, never in the repository, and with none set the dispatcher is a silent no-op, so a fresh clone runs with zero setup. /alerts/status reports whether Telegram is configured and how the most recent dispatch ended: sent, failed, or not configured. The dashboard toolbar and /watch show that state as a badge, so a silent alert leg is impossible.

When nobody gets up. The problem we set out on is the long lie, so the alert cannot be the end of the story. After a fall alert fires, the camera keeps watching. If the person is still on the ground 45 seconds later, the browser posts /incidents/escalate and a second message goes out: STILL DOWN, with the seconds elapsed and a prompt to escalate. Standing up at any point cancels it, and it fires at most once per fall. The fall alert itself carries an I am responding button; a tap stamps the alert message with who is responding so the whole care group sees one owner, the first tap wins over any later one, the dashboard shows that name within 5 seconds, and every new incident starts unacknowledged again. The location line also says where inside the flat to look: each resident's last-motion area from their own ambient sensors rides with the unit, so an alert for Rajoo reads last motion: Bedroom while one for Devi reads last motion: Kitchen. That area comes from the PIR track; the camera does not localize.

The honesty rules are tests. The scoring service carries 96 tests and the dashboard 25, and the rules above sit among them: a camera incident that claimed the accelerometer's 96.2% would fail the suite, and so would an /incidents/trace response for a fall that has no waveform. The claims in this brief are enforced by the test run rather than by policy.

The next feature: the live simulation

The demo's beat is a fall firing mid-shift. Pressing Simulate replays a real recorded SisFall fall into the same detector the caseload runs on, and it rotates through several real traces, so each press is a different body and a different waveform rather than a canned replay. Detection sends a live event down the stream; the fall pins to the top in seconds, flashes, and opens its drill-down. The waveform on screen is the actual trace the score was computed on, so the picture and the numbers agree. A second button injects a dropped phone, a spike with no free-fall or stillness, and the detector correctly stays silent, so an everyday knock never pages a caseworker.

The demo run

The demo runs two processes: the FastAPI service and the Next.js dashboard. The caseload loads calm and ranked. Press Simulate; the recorded trace runs through detect_fall, the incident arrives back over SSE, the acute row takes over the top. Only the wristband link is faked. Detection, the push, and the re-rank are the real production path.

3.Approach: problem to dashboard

The whole approach. Nothing here needs new hardware or a trained model. The home already produces the data, so we read the sensors a senior already lives with, calibrate the fall detector on public recordings, and turn the result into a ranked screen a caseworker can act on.

The problem

Alarm buttons and pendants only help while the fallen person can still press them. A senior who falls and goes unconscious presses nothing. The fall becomes a long lie, hours on the floor before anyone thinks to check.

The approach, and the hardware already exists

Ambient motion and door sensors log every kitchen visit and every door event, so a broken morning routine, or hours with no movement at all, surfaces the resident without anyone wearing or pressing anything. One optional bangle adds a second layer that catches a fall in seconds instead of hours. All of it is off-the-shelf: the Bangle.js 2 (~S$130) and Aqara motion and contact sensors (~US$20). Singapore already runs senior alert wearables at scale: GovTech's WAAS covers 26,800 seniors across 170 blocks. The hardware is already in the field; our product brings the detection and ranking that reads it.

Researching and understanding the data

We calibrated fall detection on real recordings and let each resident self-baseline from their own history. The two tracks stay separate: calibration only touches the fall track on SisFall, and the routine track is never tuned against a target. Neither track imports a foreign prior, which is why the method carries to Singapore without local training data.

Building the dashboard

The dashboard is a single ranked list, one row per resident: name, risk score, the sensor reading behind it, and a one-sentence reason. A detected fall pins to the top; the calm caseload ranks below it by how far each resident's day has drifted from their own routine. Opening a row shows the sensor facts that produced the score. Risk and confidence are separate columns, so a stale or missing sensor lowers confidence without inflating risk, and cannot manufacture a crisis. The system ranks and explains; the caseworker makes the call.

What we do not claim

  • We shrink the time it takes to notice a fall. We do not promise to catch every one.
  • Our fall thresholds were tuned on the whole SisFall set, mostly younger adults, but we measured the senior false-alarm rate separately at 18.8% on 893 elderly recordings, about half the young-adult rate. The remaining caveats are narrower: the elderly fall data is thin (63 of 75 caught, all from one volunteer), and a fall performed in a lab is not the same as a fall at home.
  • If a fall is missed, or the bangle is left on the nightstand, the resident still surfaces the slow way: hours of unusual stillness push them up the routine ranking by morning.
  • A genuinely irregular life gets wide baselines and scores less sensitively. We show that as low confidence rather than hiding it.
